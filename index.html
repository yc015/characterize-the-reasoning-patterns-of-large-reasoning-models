Your thoughts tell who you are

Characterizing the reasoning patterns of large reasoning models by building an open, LLM-proposed taxonomy of how they think.

Yida Chen, Yuning Mao, Xianjun Yang, Suyu Ge, Shengjie Bi, Lijuan Liu, Saghar Hosseini, Liang Tan, Yixin Nie, Shaoliang Nie

Harvard University · Meta Superintelligence Labs

Preprint (arXiv) Findings

Why LOT?

LOT (LLM-proposed Open Taxonomy) inductively discovers the reasoning traits that distinguish LRMs, annotates new traces with these traits, and trains a lightweight classifier to quantify how models think.

12 LRMs x 5 datasets

24,444 reasoning traces spanning GPQA-Diamond, MATH-500, AIME 24/25, CRUXEVAL, and LiveCodeBench (execution).

80–100% trace classification

LOT separates reasoning from models that differ in scale, base family, or specialization, outperforming few-shot prompting, VML, and fixed taxonomies by up to 23.8%.

Human-readable features

Reasoning differences such as circular self-checks, visualization habits, or coding-based math strategies are named and defined in natural language.

+3.3–5.7% GPQA gains

Editing smaller Qwen3 models' reasoning to emphasize useful LOT features improves answer accuracy without touching weights.

LOT in a nutshell

LOT is inspired by inductive coding in qualitative research: the taxonomy grows with the data instead of being fixed upfront.

1. Propose distinguishing traits

Llama3.3-70B compares paired traces from two LRMs solving the same problem and verbalizes reasoning differences such as premature hypothesis switching or simulated execution of code.

2. Annotate and encode

The same LLM tags new traces with the proposed traits. We create two encodings: PoR (pattern-of-reasoning, binary) and BoR (bag-of-reasoning, frequency-aware).

3. Train & expand

A logistic classifier predicts the source model. When it misclassifies a trace, LOT asks the LLM to expand the taxonomy with new features and retries, keeping the feature set open.

4. Inspectable taxonomy

The resulting feature list acts as a readable report of how two LRMs differ. These features can be aggregated, visualized, and used to guide reasoning interventions.

PoR vs. BoR encodings

PoR tracks whether a behavior appears at all in a trace. BoR retains frequency information via KNN-imputed counts, which is vital for long GPQA and AIME solutions.

What LOT reveals

Three axes—model scale, base family, and domain specialization—lead to distinct reasoning signatures.

Scaling up improves discipline

Comparing five smaller Qwen3 variants with Qwen3-32B yields 80–93% accuracy on GPQA. Larger models consistently verify their chosen theory, keep track of constraints, and avoid circular evidence loops.

  • BoR encodings add 3–14% accuracy over PoR by emphasizing how often a strategy appears.
  • Smaller models frequently restate the same fact, lose track of which law applies, and switch concepts mid-solution.
  • Even text-only models sometimes "visualize" molecules by writing structural formulas—LOT surfaces how often this happens per model.

Base models leave a fingerprint

When models share the same pretraining family (e.g., Qwen descendants), they become harder to distinguish; cross-family pairs (Phi, Mistral, Nemotron) display sharper contrasts.

  • PoR often saturates on long GPQA/AIME traces, but BoR keeps accuracy high by focusing on frequency signatures.
  • Case study: Qwen3-14B simulates Python programs on LiveCodeBench, while QwQ-32B reasons by inspecting parameters and recursion depth.

Domain training can "stick"

Seed-Coder-8B-Reasoning, tuned on coding data, occasionally solves MATH-500 problems by writing and executing Python functions. Qwen3-8B only uses code when forced to interpret diagrams.

  • LOT reveals that Seed-Coder borrows coding heuristics for 20% of math problems, compared to 2% for Qwen3-8B.
  • This inertia explains why specialized models may overfit their preferred modalities even on out-of-domain questions.

Interesting reasoning patterns identified by LOT

The qualitative taxonomy in Appendix B surfaced recurring habits that are easy to miss when only reading final answers.

Seed-Coder-8B · MATH-500

Seed-Coder defaults to code-based math reasoning

When faced with contest-math questions, Seed-Coder often writes pseudocode and executes it mentally or via Python snippets, whereas Qwen3-8B leans on human-style derivations.

    From reasoning gaps to performance gains

    LOT's features are not just descriptive—they serve as levers for editing thoughts.

    We compute odds ratios that link each reasoning feature to correctness on GPQA. Instead of asking models to obey explicit thought-level commands (which they fail to do), we summarize their original trace, edit the summary according to LOT insights, and re-expand it into a new reasoning script.

    • Encouraging verification steps and pruning redundant analysis boosts GPQA accuracy for Qwen3-0.6B/4B/8B by 3.3–5.7%.
    • Summaries keep edits within context limits (<32K tokens) and separate intervention from final-answer decoding.
    • Causal story: removing "inconsistent application of scientific principles" features reliably reduces mistakes.

    Instruction following inside the reasoning trace is brittle

    Appendix C tests whether LRMs can add a simple sentence (“I am a large language model.”) at the start of their thinking. Almost all models fail.

    • Even when the instruction only targets the first 100 reasoning words, most LRMs place the sentence elsewhere or only after reasoning finishes.
    • Qwen3-8B and Magistral-Small barely clear 50% compliance; Phi-4-RP tends to follow the instruction but inserts it in the final response instead of the thinking channel.
    • Because we cannot rely on this level of control, LOT interventions summarize, edit, and re-expand traces instead of issuing inline commands.

    Stability & dataset

    The taxonomy discovered by LOT is robust across random seeds and grounded in a large-scale reasoning corpus.

    Reasoning dataset

    • 24,444 traces sampled with official hyperparameters from open-weight LRMs.
    • Neutral codenames hide scale/family cues when prompting the annotator.
    • Train/test splits: 80/20 for GPQA, MATH-500, CRUXEVAL, LiveCodeBench; 75/25 for AIME.
    • All annotations and updates use Llama3.3-70B-Instruct with consistent sampling settings.
